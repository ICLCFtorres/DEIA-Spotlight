ICLC DEIA Spotlight

Lydia X. Z. Brown

A lawyer and organizer who helped push disability advocacy past "nothing about us without us" and into disability justice.

This spotlight is built around a SARTAC interview from the "Self-Advocacy and Beyond" series, in which self-advocate Tia Nelis speaks with Lydia X. Z. Brown, a queer, nonbinary, autistic, East Asian disability justice advocate, lawyer, and organizer. Brown uses they and them pronouns throughout. Work through the six chapters below at your own pace.

✓Chapter 1: An Advocate Who Never Chose the Title▶

Lydia X. Z. Brown describes their path into advocacy as something that was never a childhood plan. As a disabled person embedded in disability community, with a disabled spouse and disabled friends throughout their life, Brown has said it would not have been possible to move through the world and not end up advocating for it.

Brown is autistic, queer, nonbinary, and East Asian, and identifies as multiply marginalized, someone who experiences overlapping forms of discrimination while also holding certain privileges, including a legal education and lighter skinned identity as a person of color. That layered self-understanding shows up throughout their work.

✓Chapter 2: Organizing Against Institutional Violence▶

Brown's early organizing centered on some of the most severe forms of harm disabled people face. They co-founded the Washington Metro Disabled Students Collective and helped organize a protest outside the Food and Drug Administration's Maryland campus opposing the continued use of electric shock devices as behavioral modification on autistic and other developmentally disabled people.

That focus, on violence against disabled people in schools, institutions, prisons, and by police, has stayed central to Brown's work ever since, first as a member of the Autistic Self Advocacy Network's national policy team, then across a series of legal and policy roles.

✓Chapter 3: From Disability Rights to Disability Justice▶

Much of Brown's writing and public speaking has pushed the broader disability movement to think beyond legal rights alone. Brown argues that laws can force access without changing the underlying belief, still common in society, that disabled lives are burdensome or less worth living. Ableism, in that framing, is not just about ramps and accommodations, it is a set of values embedded throughout how society is organized.

This is the core idea behind the disability justice framework Brown helped popularize, one that insists disability cannot be understood on its own, separate from race, class, gender, and sexuality. A disabled Black woman and a disabled white man do not experience ableism identically, and a movement that treats disability as a single, uniform experience will fail the people facing the most compounding harm.

✓Chapter 4: Law, Policy, and the Systems That Still Exclude▶

Brown's career has moved through some of the most influential disability policy institutions in the country, including the Bazelon Center for Mental Health Law, Georgetown Law's Institute for Tech Law and Policy, and a term as chairperson of the Massachusetts Developmental Disabilities Council. Across those roles, one theme recurs: technology and institutions that appear neutral often are not.

Brown has written and spoken extensively about algorithmic bias, pointing out that automated systems used in hiring, benefits determinations, and policing frequently encode the same ableist and racist assumptions as the humans who built them, just with less accountability and less visibility.

✓Chapter 5: Mutual Aid as Advocacy▶

Brown founded the Autistic People of Color Fund to do something advocacy organizations rarely do directly, redistribute money. Through the American Association of People with Disabilities, Brown helped start a seed fund of under ten thousand dollars aimed at autistic people of color who have disproportionately faced economic deprivation and exploitation at the intersection of racism and ableism.

That fund was gone within 60 days, a fact Brown points to not as a failure but as evidence of how deep and urgent the need already was. For Brown, mutual aid and direct material support sit alongside legal and policy work as legitimate, necessary forms of advocacy, not a substitute for systemic change, but not separate from it either.

✓Chapter 6: Continuing the Work▶

Brown currently serves as Director of Public Policy at the National Disability Institute, which works to expand economic opportunity for disabled people, while continuing to lead the Autistic People of Color Fund. Over the years, Brown's advocacy has been recognized by the Obama White House, the American Association of People with Disabilities, and the National Disability Mentoring Coalition, among others.

Brown has said there is no single "biggest problem" facing disabled people today, because ableism is embedded too deeply and too broadly across society to isolate into one fix. That framing, refusing an easy, singular answer in favor of naming how many systems need to change at once, runs through everything else in Brown's work.

A Note From the DEIA Advisory Group

Lydia X. Z. Brown's work is a useful check on our own instincts as advocates. It is easy to walk into a legal case wanting to solve it for a client. Brown's framing, that disabled people are the experts on their own lives and that real advocacy starts by centering their voice and their self-determination, is a direct echo of the self-advocacy movement this spotlight is drawn from. It is worth asking, on every case, whether we are advocating for a client or alongside them.
